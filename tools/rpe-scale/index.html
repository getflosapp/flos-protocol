Why log one number after every set

Progressive overload — measurably harder sessions over weeks — is the core driver of continued adaptation in position-stand guidelines. [3] The catch: you cannot prove progression you never recorded. Logging RPE after each working set is the lowest-friction way to make effort visible and comparable across weeks — and the repetitions-in-reserve form of the scale was validated for exactly this purpose. [1][2]

It also autoregulates you: on a slept-badly day, an RPE 8 set simply means less weight — the stimulus stays on target instead of the number on the bar bullying you into junk volume or injury.

Questions, answered straight

What RPE should my working sets be?

Most strength and hypertrophy work lives at RPE 7–9 (1–3 reps in reserve). Below 7 is warm-up territory for working weights; RPE 10 is for testing, not training — failure costs recovery without extra growth stimulus.

Is RPE accurate enough to program with?

Yes — especially in the RIR form. Zourdos et al. validated the reps-in-reserve-based scale against actual proximity to failure, and experienced lifters estimate RIR within about one rep. [1][2] It gets more accurate the more consistently you log it.

Do I ever need to test my true 1RM?

No. Take any hard set — say 100 kg × 5 at RPE 8 (2 RIR, so ~7 reps to failure ≈ 81%): estimated 1RM ≈ 123 kg. Submaximal estimates are safer, recover faster, and are accurate enough to program from.
