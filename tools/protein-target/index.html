The number that does the work

In a meta-analysis of 49 studies, protein supplementation drove significantly greater gains in muscle mass and strength — and the benefit plateaued around 1.6 g/kg/day, with the 95% confidence interval reaching ~2.2 g/kg/day. [1] The ISSN position stand lands in the same neighborhood for building and maintaining muscle. [2]

Translation: your job is not finding a magic number — it is hitting a good number daily. Consistency at 1.8 g/kg beats occasional heroics at 2.5.

Why the multiplier changes by goal

In a deficit, protein is your muscle-insurance policy: higher intakes (~2.2 g/kg) help ensure the weight you lose is fat, not lean mass. Recomp sits in the middle (~2.0) — enough to build slowly while holding weight. In a controlled surplus, abundant carbs and fats spare protein, so ~1.8 g/kg covers growth comfortably.

Spread it across the day

The ISSN suggests distributing protein across the day — roughly 0.4 g/kg per meal across at least four meals is a practical pattern for maximizing muscle protein synthesis. [2] Front-loading breakfast matters more than any post-workout window anxiety.

Questions, answered straight

How much protein do I actually need?

1.6–2.2 g/kg/day covers the evidence-based range for lifters [1][2] — higher end in a deficit, lower end in a surplus. This tool picks your point on that range by goal.

Is more than 2.2 g/kg better?

The meta-analysis shows the benefit plateau [1] — beyond it you're mostly displacing carbs and fats that fuel training. Hit the number daily instead of overshooting it occasionally.

Is high protein safe?

For healthy, exercising adults, the ISSN reports no evidence of harm at these intakes. [2] Existing kidney, liver, or metabolic conditions are a different conversation — clear it with a physician or dietitian first.

Do I need protein powder?

No. Powder is convenience, not magic — whole food works identically. A shake just makes a 160 g/day target easier on a busy Tuesday. Total daily intake beats source and timing by a mile.
